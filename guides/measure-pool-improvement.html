How to measure pool improvement

Which numbers track skill · Updated August 2026

Improvement in pool is slow enough that you cannot feel it and fast enough that it is really happening. That combination is why so many players quit structured practice: the evidence exists, but nobody is writing it down. Here is what is worth measuring, and what is just noise wearing a number.

Measurements that track skill

  • Success rate on a fixed drill. Same layout, same shot count, same rules. The only honest comparison is against a previous run of the identical thing.
  • Benchmark exam score. A standardised test taken every few weeks. Its value comes entirely from not changing.
  • Consistency across sets. Three sets of 10 scoring 7/7/8 is a better game than 10/4/8, even though the totals are close. Variance drops before averages rise — it is often the first sign that anything is working.
  • Tightening targets. The same success rate on a smaller position zone is unambiguous improvement.
  • Match results against known opponents. Slow, noisy, but it is the thing you are actually training for.

Measurements that mostly track your mood

  • Hours at the table. Effort, not outcome. Useful for spotting neglected skill areas; useless as a progress metric.
  • Best-ever run. A record of your luckiest day.
  • How the session felt. Feel lags skill by weeks in both directions — players usually feel worst right after changing something correctly.
  • Success on drills you chose today. If the drill changes, the number means nothing. You will unconsciously pick drills that suit your current form.

The single most useful habit: score at the table, set by set, in the moment. Retrospective scoring is memory, and memory in pool is heavily weighted towards the last three shots.

How to read a trend without fooling yourself

Compare a block of sessions with a block of sessions — say the last four against the previous four — rather than today against last Tuesday. Single sessions swing by 20 percentage points on cloth conditions and sleep alone. If two four-session blocks differ by less than about 10 points, treat them as the same.

And separate the two questions: am I better at this drill? and am I better at pool? The first is answered by drill scores, the second only by benchmarks and games. Drills you have repeated fifty times will improve faster than your actual game, and mistaking one for the other is the most common way practice quietly stops working.

Common questions

How often should I re-take a benchmark test?

Every two to four weeks. More often and you are measuring noise; less often and you cannot tell which change caused what.

My drill scores went up but I still lose matches. Why?

Usually decisions, not execution. Drills remove the choice of which shot to play, which is the hardest part of a game. Add solo racks and score the run-outs, not the pots.

Is a success rate of 60% good?

It is meaningless without the drill and the difficulty attached. A drill you succeed at 90% of the time has stopped teaching you anything; somewhere around 60–75% is where practice is hard enough to matter and not so hard that it demoralises.
