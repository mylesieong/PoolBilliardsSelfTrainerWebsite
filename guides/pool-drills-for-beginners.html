Pool drills for beginners

Six drills, in order · Updated August 2026

A beginner's problem is not a shortage of drills — it is that every drill looks equally important. These six are ordered. Each one assumes the previous one is roughly reliable, and each has a clear signal for when you have got what you need from it.

1. The straight stop shot

Object ball on the spot, cue ball straight behind it, pot into the corner and stop the white dead. Nothing in pool is more diagnostic. If the white drifts left or right, your cue is not travelling straight; if it follows or draws, your tip height is off.

Move on when: 8 of 10 pot with the white finishing inside a ball's width of where it struck.

2. The line-up (potting under repetition)

Five balls spread along the spot line, pot them in order, replace and repeat. This builds the boring part of the game — the ability to pot the same easy ball on the twentieth attempt as well as on the first.

Move on when: you clear all five in one visit, twice in a session.

3. The cut-angle ladder

Same pocket, same object-ball position, cue ball moved progressively wider so the cut angle grows: near-straight, half-ball, thin. Most beginners have one angle they miss badly and have never identified it. This finds it.

Move on when: your success rate is within about 20 points across all three angles — not high, just even.

4. Follow and draw to a target

Pot a straight ball and bring the white to a marked spot — first with follow, then with draw. Use a coin, a chalk cube, anything physical. Judging cue-ball distance by feel is guesswork until you have seen the same shot land short and long twenty times.

Move on when: you can land within a ball's width of the target on 6 of 10 attempts, both directions.

5. Two-ball position

Two object balls. Pot the first and leave yourself on the second. This is the first drill where potting alone is not success — the shot is only good if the next one is easy. It is also the drill where most players discover they have been playing shot by shot.

Move on when: you complete both balls in 6 of 10 attempts.

6. The rail cut

Object ball frozen on or near the cushion, cut down the rail. The shot beginners avoid in games and therefore never improve at. Drilling it removes the flinch.

Move on when: it no longer feels different from any other shot.

Rule of thumb. Two drills per session, not six. A drill you did once is a demonstration; a drill you did on ten separate days is training.

Common questions

How many balls should I pot before moving to position play?

Potting and position are not sequential stages — start two-ball position as soon as stop shots are stable. Waiting until you "never miss" means waiting forever.

Should a beginner use side spin?

Not deliberately, for a while. Side spin adds throw and swerve to a stroke that is not yet repeatable, so it hides the errors you are trying to see. Follow and draw first.

How often should I practise?

Three short sessions a week beat one long one. Skill consolidates between sessions, not during them.
