How to practise pool alone

A structure for solo table time · Updated August 2026

Most solo practice fails for the same reason: there is no way to tell a good session from a bad one. You pot balls for an hour, some go in, you leave feeling either encouraged or annoyed, and nothing about your game has actually changed. The fix is not more table time. It is giving each session a shape.

1. Decide what this session is for before you chalk up

One session cannot fix everything. Pick a single focus and accept that the rest of your game is not on the agenda today:

  • Accuracy — potting the ball, repeatedly, from angles you keep missing.
  • Cue-ball control — where the white finishes, not whether the object ball drops.
  • Positioning — linking two or three shots so the next one is easy.
  • Match rhythm — full racks under something resembling pressure.

If you cannot name the focus, you are not practising; you are hitting balls. Both are fine, but only one of them shows up as improvement.

2. Warm up on a shot you cannot miss

Ten minutes of a straight stop shot down the spot line does more for the rest of the session than any complicated exercise. You are calibrating stroke and stance, not testing yourself. Do not score this part.

3. Work in sets, not in minutes

Choose a drill, choose the number of repetitions per set and the number of sets, and write both down before you start. Ten reps × three sets is a workload; "some potting practice" is not. Sets give you three separate scores from one drill, which is how you see fatigue, and how you see a real change instead of one lucky run.

Why sets matter. A single 30-shot block gives you one number and hides everything inside it. Three sets of 10 show whether you started cold, whether you faded, or whether you were simply inconsistent — three very different problems with three different fixes.

4. Score every set as you go

Potted or missed, marked at the table, before you set the balls up again. Scoring from memory at the end of a session is worthless — it records your mood, not your shooting. Two taps between racks is enough.

5. End with something that resembles a game

Drills isolate a skill; games demand you choose which skill to use. Finish with a solo rack — ghost games, or a full-table run-out attempt — so that what you drilled has a chance to appear under real decisions.

6. Test yourself on a fixed benchmark, not on today's drills

Your practice numbers drift because your practice drifts. Once every week or two, run the same standardised test — the same layout, the same rules, the same shot count — and record the score. That is the number that tells you whether three weeks of work moved anything.

Common questions

How long should a solo practice session be?

Long enough to warm up and complete one focused drill — around 30 minutes. Beyond about an hour of concentrated work, quality drops and you start grooving mistakes.

Is practising alone worse than playing people?

It is better for building skills and worse for building decisions. Solo work lets you repeat one shot fifty times, which no match ever will. You still need games; you just cannot learn technique inside them.

How do I stay motivated with no opponent?

Compete with your own record. A drill you scored 6/10 on last week is an opponent, and beating it feels the same. That only works if the scores are written down.
